Before the diagram, ask a better question.

A diagram becomes useful when everyone knows which decision it is helping them make.

Draft for owner review. An original general essay, with no identifiable project or employer details.

A box is easy to draw. Agreeing what belongs inside it takes longer. Architecture conversations can fill a whiteboard before anyone has said what needs to change, who needs it, or how the group will recognise a useful result.

Drawing can help people think. But a neat picture can also make an uncertain idea look settled. Before improving the picture, improve the question behind it.

Give the discussion a decision.

“What should the architecture be?” leaves almost everything open. A more useful question names a decision and a constraint: which boundary would let a component change independently, while keeping a particular interaction understandable?

The wording matters less than the specificity. People can compare options when they understand the trade-off. Without that, they may be answering different questions with the same vocabulary.

Make uncertainty visible.

A diagram can distinguish what is known from what is assumed. A connection might be observed, proposed or still under investigation. Those are different kinds of information and deserve different treatment.

Keeping assumptions visible creates a path to evidence. It makes it possible to ask what would change the decision, and which small investigation would be worth doing next.

Leave something another person can use.

The most useful output may be a short decision note alongside the diagram: the question, the options, the constraint that mattered and the reason for the choice.

That note gives the next person a way to understand the decision without recreating the meeting. It also leaves room for the decision to change when its assumptions do.

A clear picture helps. A clear question gives it a purpose.
